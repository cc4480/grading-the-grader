The problem with scanner accuracy

A false negative never files a complaint

Which is why almost nobody measures them.

Run a scanner against a real website and you learn one thing: what it reported. You do not learn what it walked past. The site's true fault list is unknown — that is the entire reason you ran a scanner — so there is nothing to check the output against.

This creates a quiet, comfortable failure mode for the whole industry. A scanner that reports too much gets argued with. A scanner that reports too little gets thanked for the clean bill of health. The incentives point one way, and there is no measurement pushing back.

The only way to count what a scanner misses is to point it at something whose faults you already know exactly.

So that is what we built: a deliberately vulnerable web application with every class of fault the scanner checks for, planted on purpose and catalogued. Then the real scanning engine — not a unit test, not a mock — runs against it, and every result is checked against what was actually planted. The script exits non-zero on a single miss.

Result — full active pass, credentialed, two accounts

73 of 73 tracked findings detected. Including the credentialed API surface — missing authentication, broken function-level authorisation, mass assignment and IDOR — which needs two real logged-in sessions to be checkable at all, and cross-account access control, which needs both sessions at once.

The fixture is not a set of strings shaped to satisfy our own regular expressions. The deserialization markers, for instance, are real serialized objects — a Java stream, a Ruby Marshal blob, a Python pickle and a URL-encoded PHP object — each carrying a genuine payload that decodes to the real magic bytes. A fixture that matched the marker without being the thing the marker stands for would verify our regex and nothing else.

The other half

What 111 runs against real sites actually produced

Ground truth proves the scanner finds things. Only the real world proves it does not invent them.

Forty hosts across six sectors — developer tooling, news, retail, government, productivity, communications — scanned across six rounds between 7 and 19 September 2026. Every third-party host was scanned passively only: ordinary GET requests and the headers they returned, nothing a browser does not send. The active probes ran against two domains we own.

0 Critical 47 High 210 Medium 189 Low 725 Info

Not one Critical, across all 1,171 findings. That is the expected shape for passive scans of well-run sites, and it is the number we watch hardest: a Critical appearing here, on a site that has not changed, is a scanner bug until proven otherwise.

Nearly two thirds of everything returned is Informational and scores zero toward the grade. That is deliberate. A check that can only observe whether something is advertised — rate limiting, security.txt, DNSSEC — must not move a grade, because its absence proves nothing. Scanners that let those findings count are how a well-configured site ends up with a frightening score and a customer stops trusting the tool.

The part most vendors delete

Fourteen false positives, found in our own scanner

Each one produced a wrong report on a real site. Each was root-caused, fixed, and pinned with a regression test. Here are four of them.

07 Sep

A bot-protection page, scanned as if it were the customer's site

Two sites returned a Cloudflare interstitial instead of their real page. Every content-derived check — policy, cookies, headers, fingerprint — was faithfully describing the challenge page. The report read as a scan of the customer and was a scan of a WAF error page.

08 Sep

The most email-secure domain we scanned, reported as having no email security

A helper derived the email domain by counting label lengths, so it looked up SPF and DMARC at hostnames that do not exist. Separately, a DNS helper returned every record in the answer regardless of type, so two CNAMEs answering an MX query read as “this domain actively sends email” and escalated the finding. Three bugs behind one High. The helper was deleted rather than patched.

08 Sep

“No rate limiting detected” — on 15 of 30 sites

A passive GET to a homepage cannot observe rate limiting. It lives on login and API routes, and CDNs throttle silently. We had already patched the exception list twice, which is the shape of a check chasing an unobservable property one vendor at a time. Renamed to what it can actually see, and dropped to zero weight.

08 Sep

A fix that introduced the bug it was fixing

The interstitial detector matched a header by presence. That vendor sets the header on traffic it lets through. The real 1.3 MB homepage had been in the response body the whole time. The same error — reading the presence of a response as the meaning of the response — reappeared inside the fix for a previous instance of itself.

A scanner with no published false-positive history has either never looked, or has looked and not told you.

The full audit runs to five rounds and is kept as a working document, not a marketing artefact. The fifth round — the first to sweep the active probes for false positives at all — found none, and says so plainly rather than claiming a milestone.

Restraint as a feature

Four things this scanner deliberately will not do

  • It will not grade a site it could not actually read. When a bot-protection layer answers instead of the site, the scan is reported as withheld, with a coverage note. The alternative is handing out clean A grades to sites nobody looked at — indistinguishable, on the page, from genuinely well-configured ones.
  • It will not score you down for what it cannot observe. Informational findings carry zero weight. If the only honest statement is “this is not advertised in the response headers”, that is what the finding says.
  • It will not run active probes without verified ownership. Injection, traversal, authorisation and SSRF testing require a verified domain. Everything else is passive.
  • It will not tell you that you are compliant. Findings map to SOC 2, PCI DSS, ISO 27001 and OWASP ASVS controls, because that mapping is real, checkable work. Only a licensed CPA firm issues a SOC 2 report and only a QSA signs off PCI. The mapping is evidence for an auditor, never a verdict.

Check it yourself

The evidence is reproducible

Every claim above comes from a script in the repository, and the raw output of every run is committed beside the summary. Nothing here is a number recalled from memory. 

# Detection: point the real engine at the fixture with known faults.
# Exits non-zero on a single miss.
npx tsx artifacts/api-server/scripts/verify-vuln-fixture.ts

# False positives: sweep the guarded corpus and read the output.
npx tsx artifacts/api-server/scripts/live-scan.ts --json sweep.json

The instruction we follow ourselves is to read that output rather than diff it. The audit exists because six findings were once “fixed” on suspicion and all six turned out to be real.

Where this stops

What this evidence does not claim

No head-to-head comparison has been run

SecScan has not been benchmarked against Nessus, Burp Suite, ZAP, Nuclei, Detectify or any other scanner. We are not going to tell you we beat tools we have never run. What is on this page is our own evidence, measured against ground truth we control and published in full.

It is also an outside view, taken repeatedly. SecScan does not read your source repository, your CI pipeline or your dependency manifests. A vulnerable package installed but never reachable from outside will not be seen by an external scanner, and that includes this one. It is not a replacement for SAST, for dependency scanning, or for a penetration test by a human being.

And the fixture measures what the fixture contains. Three findings in one module are not planted in it yet, and that is recorded in the verification log rather than rounded away.

Ask any scanner you are considering for its detection rate against a target with known ground truth, and for its false-positive history. Then compare the answers to this page.
