01 — the finding

One missing header explains most of it

Of 60 high-severity findings across all 100 sites, 39 are a single check: no Content-Security-Policy header at all. Nothing else comes close — the runner-up appears three times. CSP is the browser's main defence against cross-site scripting, and two fifths of the most-visited sites on the internet ship without one.

The sharpest example. CNN does send a policy, which reads as diligence until you look at it: script-src 'unsafe-eval' 'unsafe-inline' 'self' *. The bare * permits scripts from any origin, and the two unsafe- values disable what protection would remain. The header is present and does nothing.

04 — method

How this was measured, and what it is not

Passive only. Response headers, TLS configuration, DNS and email authentication records. Equivalent to loading the page and running public DNS lookups — the same basis SSL Labs and securityheaders.com have published on for years. No authentication, no injection, nothing a browser would not already fetch.

Hand-verified. Every critical and high-severity finding was reproduced manually before it appeared here. That process caught eight distinct false-positive classes in our own scanner, each fixed before this data was collected: a formatting template inside a crypto library read as a leaked private key, a CDN's host-routing error read as a subdomain takeover, third-party cookies attributed to the site that merely loaded them, and a public single-page-app config read as an exposure. Several sites had their grade corrected as a direct result.

What a missing header is not. A missing Content-Security-Policy is not a breach and does not mean a site is compromised. It means one layer of defence against cross-site scripting is absent. These grades measure published security posture — the part anyone can check — not the security of the systems behind it.

Reconnaissance. Alongside the grades, 48 of the sites had their external surface mapped: 1646 subdomains and 2247 DNS records, via certificate-transparency logs and public resolution.
