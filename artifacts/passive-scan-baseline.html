Passive Scan Baseline
SecScan · Research report

What passive scanning actually finds on 30 well-run websites

We ran SecScan's passive pipeline against a fixed sample of thirty prominent websites across six sectors and recorded every finding. Zero critical issues were found, and fifteen high-severity ones. The most common results are not vulnerabilities at all but absent hardening headers: cross-origin isolation is missing on 23 of 23 fully observed sites, and DNSSEC on 15 of 23. Every finding below can be reproduced by a reader with curl or dig, and the commands are given. The run also found seven false positives in our own scanner, which we fixed before publishing; those are documented here rather than quietly removed, because a baseline is only worth as much as the instrument that produced it.

1 · Method

What was scanned, and how

The sample was fixed before any scanning began: thirty sites, six sectors, five per sector. Stating the frame in advance is what makes a prevalence claim meaningful rather than a collection of anecdotes.

SectorSites
Developer platformsgithub.com, gitlab.com, npmjs.com, stackoverflow.com, vercel.com
Security & infrastructurecloudflare.com, mozilla.org, digitalocean.com, fastly.com, letsencrypt.org
News & mediabbc.co.uk, nytimes.com, theguardian.com, reuters.com, apnews.com
Commerce & paymentsshopify.com, stripe.com, etsy.com, ebay.com, amazon.co.uk

Passive only

Every scan ran with active probing disabled. That means HTTP GETs of the homepage and pages linked from it, plus DNS lookups — no payloads, no parameter manipulation, no authentication attempts, nothing that alters state. This is the only mode that is appropriate against sites you do not own, and it has a second benefit for a paper: everything it observes is a public fact that a reader can check independently.

Three populations, reported separately

Of the thirty sites in the frame, 23 were fully observed, 6 returned a bot-protection interstitial instead of their own page, and 1 was unreachable. Prevalence statistics in this paper are computed over the 23, and never over 29, because the six intercepted sites cannot contribute a denominator for a header they were never observed to send.

Why the intercepted sites look "clean" and are not

npmjs.com, stackoverflow.com, reuters.com, etsy.com, ebay.com and amazon.co.uk each answered our request with a Cloudflare challenge page. The scanner detects this and withholds every finding derived from the response, so those six show only 2–4 findings each and zero actionable ones.

That is suppressed coverage, not a clean bill of health. Read naively, this data would rank Amazon the most secure site in the corpus. It means only that we never saw Amazon's page. Which sites get challenged also varies between runs — amazon.co.uk was fully observed on one earlier run and intercepted on the final one.

letsencrypt.org was unreachable from our test vantage point on three attempts; plain curl also timed out, so the host was not reachable rather than the scan failing. It is excluded, and the Security & infrastructure sector is therefore n=4.

2 · Results

What was found

Across the 23 fully observed sites: 286 findings, 116 actionable (severity above Info), a mean of 12.4 findings and 5.0 actionable per site. No critical findings on any site in the sample.

06121823

Number of the 23 fully observed sites carrying each finding. Bars share one scale, 0–23.

The headline is what is absent, not what is broken

The three most common findings are the cross-origin isolation headers — COEP on all 23 sites, CORP on 21, COOP on 18. These are Info severity and contribute nothing to a grade, correctly: they are a modern hardening layer that most of the web has not adopted, and their absence is not a vulnerability. The same is true of DNSSEC, absent on 15 of 23 including github.com, stripe.com and wikipedia.org.

The findings that carry real weight are narrower. Eight of 23 sites send no Content-Security-Policy at all, and a further 11 send one weakened by unsafe-inline or unsafe-eval in script-src — so only four sites in the sample ship a CSP that meaningfully constrains script execution.

Three worth commenting on

The Cloudflare and Vercel findings are inconsistency, not absence. Both send a Content-Security-Policy on their root document and omit it on localised routes — /es-es/, /fr-fr/, /ja-jp/ and others. That is a more interesting result than a site with no policy at all, because it is the kind of gap that appears when a header is applied at one layer of a stack and not another.

archive.org's exposed source map is real but arguably over-severe at High. The map covers a vendored lit polyfill, so what it exposes is a public library's source rather than the operator's own code. We are reporting it at the severity the scanner assigned rather than quietly downgrading it in the write-up, but a reader should weigh it accordingly.

gitlab.com redirects to a marketing site. Requesting https://gitlab.com lands on about.gitlab.com, so both GitLab findings describe that site and say nothing about the GitLab application. Five other sites in the sample also redirected across hosts or paths; the appendix lists the final URL for each.

4 · Instrument

Seven false positives we found in our own scanner

This run was intended to produce a baseline. It also produced a false-positive audit, because the first pass reported things that were not true. All seven were fixed and covered by regression tests before the data in this paper was captured. We are publishing them because a measurement is only as good as the instrument, and a vendor who has never found a fault in their own scanner has not looked.

The one that mattered most

Our first run reported GOV.UK as having neither an SPF nor a DMARC record, both at High severity. GOV.UK publishes v=spf1 -all at gov.uk and p=reject at _dmarc.gov.uk — close to the strongest email authentication posture a UK domain can have.

That single finding contained three separate bugs, and it is trivially falsifiable: dig +short TXT gov.uk. Publishing it would have destroyed the credibility of everything else on this page.

  1. Email records were looked up on the wrong name

    The registrable domain was derived by counting label lengths. For www.gov.uk, "uk" is two characters and "gov" is three, so it took the .co.uk branch and then returned the hostname unchanged — querying www.gov.uk for SPF and _dmarc.www.gov.uk for DMARC. Neither exists. www.bbc.co.uk escaped only by having a fourth label.

  2. DNS answers were counted without checking their type

    A DNS answer section carries the whole resolution chain, not just the record type you asked for. An MX query for www.gov.uk returns two CNAMEs pointing at Fastly and no MX at all. Counting them as mail servers told the scanner the domain "actively sends email", which escalated the already-wrong finding from Medium to High.

  3. Evidence claimed a DNS status the resolver never returned

    The DMARC evidence string hardcoded Status: NOERROR (domain exists). _dmarc.www.gov.uk is NXDOMAIN. The function's own documentation already said a missing-record finding required a NOERROR status; the code never checked.

  4. DNSSEC was checked at the wrong name, and the fix for bug 2 exposed it

    DNSKEY records live at the zone apex. Asking a CNAMEd www host returns the CNAME chain and no DNSKEY: www.nasa.gov answers with one CNAME while nasa.gov is properly signed. Before answers were filtered by type, that CNAME was counted as a DNSKEY and the check passed for entirely the wrong reason — a hidden false negative that fixing bug 2 turned into a visible false positive. Corpus-wide, DNSSEC findings went from 13/30 to 26/29 in one run, which is what made it obvious. The higher figure is the correct one: the old statistic was under-reporting.

  5. A check asserted something a passive scan cannot observe

    "No Rate Limiting Detected" fired on 15 of 30 sites including Stripe, Wikipedia and GOV.UK. Stripe sends no rate-limit headers at all and unquestionably rate-limits; the protection lives on login and API routes, not the homepage, and CDNs throttle silently. The check could see whether rate limiting was advertised and nothing more. Renamed to say exactly that, and dropped to Info with zero weight in the grade.

  6. A publishable vendor key was reported as a leaked credential

    nytimes.com was reported at High for a "hardcoded JWT" that is a survey widget's apiKey inside Google Tag Manager, with payload {"company_id":"…","iat":…}. Tokens now split on whether the payload carries a principal claim — sub, user_id, email, role, scope. With one it is a credential leak; with none it is an account identifier that vendor SDKs publish deliberately.

  7. Another company's error page was reported under the customer's name

    When a bot-protection interstitial answers instead of the origin, every check that reads the response describes that interstitial. Etsy's report said the site was missing CSP and serving a wildcard CORS policy; both were true only of Cloudflare's challenge page, and twelve of eighteen findings described a page Etsy never served. Findings are now withheld unless their source never read the intercepted response.

One error, five times

Bugs 1 through 4, and two earlier ones in this scanner's history, are all the same mistake: treating the presence of a response as the meaning of the response. Reachability read as an answer. A vendor header's presence read as "blocked" when it is set on allowed traffic. Evidence fixed regardless of what came back. A CNAME counted as an MX record. NXDOMAIN read as "unknown" when it is the ordinary way of saying "no record".

Twice during this work the same error was committed while fixing it — the first attempt at bug 3's fix normalised NXDOMAIN to NOERROR so one status check could serve two callers, which made the evidence assert that a non-existent name existed. That is the class of defect worth naming publicly, because recognising the shape is the only thing that catches the next one.

Measured effect of the fixes

Measured across the 29 sites common to both runs, actionable findings fell from 168 to 117 and total findings from 361 to 303. gov.uk went from 9 findings and 3 actionable to 7 and 0; etsy.com from 17 and 10 to 4 and 1; stackoverflow.com from 6 and 1 to 3 and 0.

Those 51 are not all "false positives removed", and it would be convenient to imply otherwise. The reduction is three different things: findings deleted because they were wrong (GOV.UK's two, the NYT token), findings withheld because they described a bot-protection page rather than the customer, and one check reclassified from Low to Info because it cannot observe what it was asserting — that last one still appears in every report, it simply no longer counts as actionable.

5 · Limitations

What this data does not show

  • One vantage point, one moment. All scans ran from a single network location on 8 September 2026. Sites serve different responses by geography and change configuration without notice; these numbers will drift.
  • Homepages, not applications. A passive scan sees the public front door and pages linked from it. Nothing here describes authenticated areas, APIs, or the parts of these products where the interesting security work actually happens.
  • Absence of a finding is not evidence of security. Active testing — injection, access control, SSRF — was disabled throughout, and those flaws were not looked for. A site with zero actionable findings here has not been shown to be safe.
  • Six sites were only partially observed and are excluded from every prevalence denominator. Their low finding counts reflect suppressed coverage, not good configuration.
  • Severity is our scanner's judgement. Where we think a severity is arguable — archive.org's vendored source map at High — we have said so rather than adjusting it silently.
  • Headers are not the whole story. A missing header is cheap to detect and cheap to fix, which is exactly why it dominates this kind of result. It is a weak proxy for how secure an organisation actually is.

Scans were passive: HTTP GETs and DNS lookups against publicly served pages, with all active probing disabled. No authentication was attempted, no parameters were manipulated, and no state was altered on any site.

Named organisations appear only as scan targets. Nothing here describes a vulnerability, an exploit, or a breach in any of them. Absent hardening headers are configuration choices these operators are entitled to make, and several — notably GOV.UK, which our scanner initially defamed and which turned out to have among the strongest email authentication in the sample — are examples of good practice rather than poor.

Findings are a point-in-time observation from a single network vantage point on 8 September 2026 and will not reproduce exactly as these sites change.
